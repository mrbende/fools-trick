# Argumentative prose

Two jobs, and they are separate. **Structure** decides what the reader assembles and in what order.
**Voice** decides how each sentence lands. This skill is the first. `writing-style` is the second, and
a draft can have perfect sentences and still fail because it never ascends.

Read §1 to understand the mode, §2–4 to build the arc, §5–6 to build a subsection, §7 as a filter.

---

## 1. The mode: an ascending spiral, not a syllogism

The default technical mode argues from assumed premises to incontestably deduced conclusions. With
the infallible and often meaningless logic of the algebraic equation. It convinces the analytic
faculty and nothing else, and it fails on exactly the arguments worth making, because the interesting
claims are ones no single deduction reaches.

The alternative, stated by John Freeman describing Jung's method:

> "They convince not by means of the narrowly focused spotlight of the syllogism, but by skirting, by
> repetition, by presenting a recurring view of the same subject seen each time from a slightly
> different angle — until suddenly the reader who has never been aware of a single, conclusive moment
> of proof finds that he has unknowingly embraced and taken into himself some wider truth."
>
> "The arguments spiral upward over the subject like a bird circling a tree. At first, near the
> ground, it sees only a confusion of leaves and branches. Gradually, as it circles higher and
> higher, the recurring aspects of the tree form a wholeness and relate to their surroundings."

**The load-bearing word is upward.** A bird circling a tree at constant height is not spiralling, it
is repeating, and repetition without altitude is the failure mode this skill exists to prevent. Each
pass must see more of the tree *and* more of what surrounds it.

Three consequences to hold onto:

- **No single conclusive moment.** Do not hunt for the one paragraph that proves the thesis. Build
  six passes that each make it harder to believe anything else. The reader should finish unable to
  locate where they were convinced.
- **Deliberate return is structure, not redundancy.** Meeting the same object in four sections is
  correct provided each return comes from a new direction and at greater height.
- **The early passes may look like a confusion of leaves.** That is tolerable for a few pages and not
  for twenty. Signpost the altitude so the reader knows they are climbing.

## 2. Organize around big ideas, not around work performed

The first draft of any structure is a list of what you did. That is a lab notebook. The reader wants
ideas, and your experiments are evidence for ideas rather than the ideas themselves.

**The conversion.** Write out everything you did. Then ask of each item: *what idea does this make
believable?* Group by the answer. Several experiments collapsing into one idea is the normal and
desired outcome, and an experiment attaching to no idea is either a supplement or a cut.

| what you did | the idea it serves |
|---|---|
| three controls, two nulls, a calibration block | *the instrument can report a zero* |
| a size sweep, a preregistered slope | *the effect grows with the substrate, predictably* |
| six correlations, two permutation tests | *what predicts is a relation, not a property* |

Sections are named for the **idea**. If a section title names a method or a block, it is not yet a
section.

## 3. The altitude ladder: order the ideas so the argument ascends

Once the ideas exist, order them by altitude. Altitude is **how much of the world the idea speaks
to**, not how hard the work was or how confident you are.

Rank each idea on three axes, then order by their sum:

| axis | ground level | high altitude |
|---|---|---|
| **scope** | a fact about this system | a condition on a class of systems |
| **surprise** | confirms what was expected | reverses what the field assumes |
| **reach** | matters inside the subfield | a non-specialist can see why it matters |

**The resulting shape, which is near-universal:**

1. **It exists, and here it is.** The object before any statistic. Cheap to accept, and it buys the
   reader's attention for everything that follows.
2. **It is not an artifact of how we looked.** The instrument, the controls, the nulls. Dull to write
   and load-bearing for the rest; put it early precisely *because* everything downstream is divided
   by it.
3. **It behaves lawfully.** Dose-response, a predicted magnitude, a scaling. This is where the
   argument stops being anecdote.
4. **Here is what sets it.** Mechanism. The first pass a reader will quote.
5. **Here is where it stops.** Limits, capacity, the dial that turns it off. Limits raise altitude
   rather than lowering it, because a bounded claim is a stronger claim.
6. **Here is the thing we got wrong, and what it cost.** Optional and powerful. A correction placed
   late reads as confidence; placed early it reads as apology.
7. **Here is the largest thing this implies.** The finish. Highest scope, highest surprise, and it
   should recast something the reader believed on the way in.

**Rules for the ladder.**

- **End on the biggest idea, never on the most recent work.** Chronology is not altitude. The thing
  you ran last is usually not the thing that matters most.
- **The finish must be a claim about the world, not about the paper.** "We showed our method wins" is
  ground level. "Adding a competent member to a collective can *reduce* its competence, and you can
  predict when from a table" is altitude.
- **Never bury a reversal.** If one finding overturns an assumption, it goes at or near the end where
  the reader has the context to feel it.
- **Put the dull-but-necessary early.** Instrument, calibration, controls. A reader forgives
  foundations in section two and resents them in section seven.
- **Check monotonicity at the level of ideas, not titles.** Read only your section titles in order.
  Every *idea group* must be contiguous and larger than the group before it. Within a group, section
  sizes vary and that is texture rather than a defect: a small result completing a large idea belongs
  beside its siblings. Run the strict title-by-title version first anyway, because a dip is the
  cheapest signal that two sections serving one idea have been separated.
- **Load-bearing methodology is exempt.** An idea the whole argument is divided by goes where the
  reader needs it, which is early, even when its altitude says late. Do not fight this: place it
  early and **claim its altitude in the Discussion**, where you argue it is the transferable
  contribution. A calibration section in position three and a "what transfers" beat in the Discussion
  is the correct handling of a high-altitude foundation.

## 4. Signpost the altitude

The spiral fails when a reader cannot tell they are climbing. Three cheap devices:

- **A roadmap of one clause per section**, in Results order, at the end of the Introduction.
- **An explicit escalation sentence** at the hinge: *"Everything so far is about a pair. The rest of
  the paper asks whether a pair is also the largest unit needed."*
- **Closing each section on what it licenses**, not on what it found. The last sentence of a section
  is a rung, so it should point up.

## 5. The subsection formula

Every Results subsection contains four things. This is what it must *contain*, not the order its
sentences must appear in.

**1. A title that is a claim with a verb**, understandable and memorable to anyone.

> not "Scheduler independence" → **"The pattern survives a deterministic scheduler."**
> not "Composition analysis" → **"The effect needs two populations, and peaks when they are equal."**
> not "Ablation study" → **"Removing the gate changes nothing; removing direction removes everything."**

Test: could a reader who skimmed only your titles reconstruct the argument? If yes, the titles are
doing their job. If the titles are noun phrases, the reader gets a table of contents instead of an
argument.

**2. In order to learn X, we took approach Y.** The question in the reader's terms, then the move.
Never open on the method: a method with no question attached is a procedure.

**3. We performed experiment Z, observing W.** Statistics inline, with the unit of analysis named.
Numbers live inside sentences and carry their scope.

**4. Therefore we conclude ABC.** What follows, *and what does not*. The second half is where
credibility is made.

**Do not let the formula become a stamp.** Four subsections opening "To test this, we…" reads as a
filled-in form. Vary the entry: open on the question, the result, the objection, or the one case that
breaks the pattern. The formula governs content, not word order.

## 6. Figures carry the claims

**Aim for 8 to 10 figures in a main text.** Beyond that no single figure carries a claim and the
reader stops reading them as arguments.

- **A figure title is a claim with a verb**, in the same register as its subsection, and generally
  matching it. One figure, one claim.
- **Every section owns at least one figure.** A section with none is under-argued; two sections
  sharing one means one of them is not a section.
- **A figure that only illustrates gets cut or demoted.** If the caption can be replaced by a
  sentence, replace it and reclaim the slot.
- **If a figure changed a claim rather than illustrating one, say so.** That is a finding about the
  analysis and it belongs in the text.

## 7. The filter

Run before shipping a structure.

**Structural failures**

- **The list.** Sections are "Experiment 1, Experiment 2, Ablations". No ideas, only work. → §2.
- **The plateau.** Every section is the same size. Nothing builds. → §3, check monotonicity.
- **The split idea.** Two sections serving one idea separated by a section serving another, so the
  reader meets a dip. The strict title test catches it; the fix is to make the group contiguous
  rather than to inflate the smaller section.
- **The anticlimax.** The paper ends on a robustness check or on whatever ran last. → §3.
- **The buried lede.** The biggest claim is in section three and everything after is smaller.
- **The premature summit.** The abstract and Introduction give away the finish, so the spiral has
  nowhere to ascend. Preview the *question*, not the payoff.
- **Repetition without altitude.** A pass that adds no number, comparison, consequence, or angle. Cut
  it and cite the earlier pass.
- **The unset hook.** A framework named in the Background and never used again is a debt.
- **One claim, two homes.** Every claim is stated once at full strength in the section whose job it
  is; every other mention is a pointer. When the same sentence appears twice, the later one loses it.
- **A result that debuts downstream.** Any finding whose numbers appear first in a Discussion or a
  Conclusion has never been written down under the scrutiny its own section would apply. Test with a
  set difference: numeric literals in the downstream section that appear in neither the setup nor the
  results. In one manuscript this returned four results, and writing the first of them into its
  proper section revealed that the block's data said the *opposite* of what the Discussion had
  claimed, because the plan had been drafted before the arms were read.
- **The setup section spends the payoff section.** The specific and most damaging case: a Methods or
  Background section that gives the measured value has already spent the Results section that owns it,
  and the reader arrives at the finding having been told. Both sections read well alone and the paper
  still deflates. **Setup specifies; payoff measures.** Detect it by diffing numeric literals between
  the two: any number in both is a defect unless it is a definition.

**Content failures**

- **A conclusion the evidence does not reach.** The ABC clause overshoots the W clause. Most common
  in the finish, where the temptation to ascend is strongest.
- **A title that outruns its number.** A claim-title asserting more than the subsection measures.
  Check every title against its own statistics; a title saying "costs nothing" over a result
  distinguishable from zero is a title that has to change.
- **Shape words unchecked.** *Climbs, rises, monotone, sharpens, doubles* attached to a series that
  dips or falls short of the ratio claimed. Reconstruct the series before using the word.
- **A limit presented as an apology.** Limits are altitude. State them as findings.

## 8. The intelligibility filter

When the argument is technical and the audience is not only technical, a second role runs alongside
the writer's: **filter for intelligibility, not for interpretation.** Freeman's description of his own
job on *Man and His Symbols* is the model — he made the argument legible to non-experts without
becoming its interpreter.

In practice:

- **The object before the framework.** A reader meets the concrete thing and watches it behave before
  meeting the vocabulary that names it.
- **Every abstraction cashed on arrival.** Coin a term once, define it on the spot, then rely on it.
  A coinage earns its place by being used more than twice and doing work no existing phrase does.
- **One image per section, and the literal claim immediately after.** Metaphor is welcome on two
  conditions: it is cashed, and it is rare enough to land.
- **The non-specialist test, applied to titles only.** If your section titles are intelligible to a
  smart reader outside the field, the argument's skeleton is sound even where its muscles are
  technical.
- **Do not simplify the claim to simplify the sentence.** Filtering for intelligibility means
  removing needless difficulty, never removing precision. When the two conflict, keep precision and
  spend a sentence on the difficulty.

## 9. Worked example: an escalation that ends on the world

A study of co-resident computation in self-sorting arrays. Nine ideas, ordered by altitude, titles
only:

1. A clean-room corpus reproduces both phenomena, and one run shows the pattern arrive and leave.
2. A detector calibrated on an empty carrier is what lets this paper report a zero.
3. The pattern strengthens with the array at a slope we wrote down before running it.
4. Only the distributed collective builds it: a central coordinator running the same moves produces none.
5. What predicts the pattern is always a rate, never a distance.
6. The pattern costs its host under three parts in a thousand, and no cell can perceive it.
7. Arrival destroys the pattern, and giving the host slack brings it back.
8. The parent study's second competency is real, and needs the defect they ran second.
9. **Three policies decompose into pairs, and freeze more often than two.**

Read the titles alone: the argument is reconstructible, each is a claim with a verb, and the sizes
increase. The finish is a claim about collectives rather than about sorting, it reverses the
intuition that adding a competent member helps, and a non-specialist can see why it matters. The
correction sits at 8, late enough to read as confidence.

Note what is *not* last: the size sweep (3) is the most quantitatively satisfying result and the
calibration (2) took the most work. Neither is the biggest idea, so neither ends the paper.

**A worked example goes stale, and this one did.** Item 5 read "a relation between two agents, never
a property of either" for as long as the paper did, and the paper's own strongest association turned
out to be a *level* — a tempo the two agents share, which is a property they have in common rather
than a relation between them. A second pass found the replacement wrong in the other direction:
"never a distance" held for three of four clearing predictors and the fourth was a reach measured
against progress. Both defects passed every mechanical check, because a title that overstates its
subsection is a claim about a number and not about a string. **Check a claim-title against the
statistics it summarises, not against the memory of them**, and re-check a skill's examples whenever
the work they were generalised from moves.
